# ITER Daily Check-In Survey

## Purpose

This document proposes a warm, conversational daily check-in for ITER. The questions are designed to collect information that may be useful when monitoring emotional health and a person's response to treatment without making the experience feel like a formal medical questionnaire.

The wording should feel as though a thoughtful friend is checking in. It should be calm, personal, specific, and free of judgment.

> **Suggested introduction**
>
> Hey, how has today been for you? Move the slider to the place that feels closest. There are no right or wrong answers, and you can skip anything you do not want to answer.

## Answer format

The app will **not present fixed numbered answer buttons**. Each standard question will use a **continuous slider from 0 to 10**.

- The patient can place the slider anywhere from `0` to `10`.
- The two ends of the slider should have short, question-specific descriptions.
- A short description may also appear near the middle to make the scale easier to understand.
- The selected number can be displayed above the slider while it is being moved.
- For consistency, a higher score should always represent a more noticeable symptom, difficulty, or concern.
- The slider should begin in a neutral, unanswered state rather than defaulting to a number.
- Selecting **Skip** should be different from selecting `0`.

Example:

```text
Not at all                                      Extremely
0 ───────────────────────●────────────────────────── 10
```

Medication and safety responses require additional care. A slider may be used as the first interaction, but it should not replace clear follow-up questions when the response suggests a missed dose or a possible safety concern.

## Daily check-in questions

### 1. Mood

**Thinking about today, how heavy or low did your mood feel?**

Slider: `0 — Not at all` to `10 — Extremely`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Mood naturally changes, but the intensity and duration of a low mood can be clinically important. Following it over time helps the care team see whether a change is temporary or part of a longer pattern, including around treatment changes.

### 2. Enjoyment

**Did the things you normally enjoy feel flat or less enjoyable today?**

Slider: `0 — They felt as enjoyable as usual` to `10 — Nothing felt enjoyable`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Enjoyment can change separately from sadness. A sustained loss of interest or pleasure—sometimes called *anhedonia*—can be an important sign of how emotional health and treatment response are changing.

### 3. Sleep

**How much did last night's sleep get in your way today—because it was too little, too much, or interrupted?**

Slider: `0 — Not at all` to `10 — It affected me throughout the day`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Both too little and too much sleep can affect mood, memory, energy, and appetite. Sleep changes may also appear after starting a medicine or changing a dose.

### 4. Appetite

**Did eating feel different today—less appealing than usual, or harder to stop once you started?**

Slider: `0 — My appetite felt normal` to `10 — It felt completely different`

If the slider is above `0`, show a short follow-up:

> Did you mostly feel like eating **less**, eating **more**, or did it **change during the day**?

#### Why are we asking you this?

Appetite can change with mood, sleep, stress, physical health, and medication. Tracking both the direction and intensity helps the care team understand possible effects on nutrition, weight, energy, and treatment tolerance.

### 5. Tension

**How much tension did you carry today—like tight shoulders, a clenched jaw, a knot in your stomach, or feeling unable to settle?**

Slider: `0 — None` to `10 — Extreme tension`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Tension is one way the nervous system responds to worry or stress. New or increasing tension can also occur with sleep disruption, medication effects, or physical restlessness, so the pattern provides useful clinical context.

### 6. Reading or watching something

**If you tried to read a book, watch a film, or follow a show today, how much effort did it take?**

Slider: `0 — It felt easy` to `10 — I could not manage it`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Following a story uses attention, memory, processing speed, and mental energy. When a familiar activity suddenly requires much more effort, it can reveal cognitive fatigue that a general mood question might miss.

### 7. Calls and messages

**How much did you find yourself avoiding phone calls or putting off replies, even from people you usually feel comfortable with?**

Slider: `0 — Not at all` to `10 — I avoided nearly all of them`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Responding to people requires social energy, attention, and motivation. A change in communication habits can provide an early sign of withdrawal, mental overload, or difficulty managing everyday demands.

### 8. Digestion

**How much did digestive problems bother you today—such as nausea, stomach discomfort, constipation, diarrhea, or indigestion?**

Slider: `0 — Not at all` to `10 — Extremely`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Digestive symptoms can be connected to food, stress, illness, or medication. Their timing is especially useful when assessing medication tolerance, hydration, nutrition, and whether a symptom appeared after a treatment change.

### 9. Irritability

**How easily did small things get under your skin today?**

Slider: `0 — No more than usual` to `10 — Almost everything irritated me`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Irritability can change even when sadness does not. It may be connected to mood, anxiety, poor sleep, pain, increased activation, or medication effects, and it can influence relationships and daily functioning.

### 10. Losing track of tasks

**How often did you start doing something and then forget what you were doing or leave it unfinished without meaning to?**

Slider: `0 — Never` to `10 — With almost every task`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Keeping track of an activity uses working memory and executive function. Difficulties here can affect cooking, driving, work, appointments, and taking medication safely.

### 11. Physical exhaustion

**How physically drained did your body feel today—even after sitting down or resting?**

Slider: `0 — Not drained` to `10 — Completely exhausted`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Physical exhaustion can be influenced by sleep, emotional strain, medication, illness, pain, or reduced activity. Following it alongside other symptoms helps identify patterns and changes that may need a physical-health review.

### 12. Following conversations

**When someone was talking to you today, how often did their words feel hard to follow, as though your mind was foggy or a step behind?**

Slider: `0 — Never` to `10 — During almost every conversation`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Following a conversation depends on attention, memory, and processing speed. Mental fog can affect communication and may change with sleep, stress, medication, or overall emotional and physical health.

### 13. Concentration

**When you needed to focus today, how hard was it to keep your attention on one thing without your mind drifting?**

Slider: `0 — It was easy` to `10 — I could not focus`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Concentration affects work, study, reading, driving, and decision-making. Tracking it separately from memory helps clarify which part of thinking feels difficult and whether it is changing over time.

### 14. Seeing friends

**If a friend had invited you out today, how hard would it have felt to go—even if part of you wanted to?**

Slider: `0 — Not hard at all` to `10 — It would have felt impossible`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Spending time with others depends on energy, motivation, confidence, and enjoyment. A growing gap between wanting connection and being able to act on it can be an important change to notice.

### 15. Everyday basics

**How much effort did basic things take today—like showering, getting dressed, preparing food, or tidying up?**

Slider: `0 — They felt manageable` to `10 — I could not manage them`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Everyday functioning helps show how strongly symptoms are affecting a person's life. It can also reveal improvement that may not yet appear in an overall mood score.

### 16. Feeling unusually “revved up”

**Did you feel unusually energized or “revved up” today—like needing much less sleep, having racing thoughts, talking much more, or acting more impulsively than usual?**

Slider: `0 — Not at all` to `10 — Extremely`

#### Why are we asking you this?

A sudden increase in energy or activity can be important, particularly when it appears with reduced need for sleep, racing thoughts, or impulsive behavior. These changes may require prompt review after starting or adjusting medication.

### 17. Safety

**Since your last check-in, how strong were any thoughts about self-harm?**

Slider: `0 — I did not have these thoughts` to `10 — I may be in immediate danger`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Thoughts about death or suicide can change quickly and may not be visible to other people. Asking directly helps the app offer immediate support and helps determine when contact with a trained professional may be needed.

The slider must **not** be the only safety assessment. Any answer above `0`, an unanswered safety question, or concerning changes elsewhere in the survey should trigger direct, clinically approved follow-up questions. A response suggesting current or immediate danger must activate the app's emergency safety pathway rather than waiting for a later report.

### 18. Tension
Today, how much tension did you notice in your jaw or neck—like clenching your teeth, holding your shoulders tight, or finding it hard to relax those muscles?

Slider: `0 — Not at all` to `10 — Extremely`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Jaw and neck tension can be connected to stress, worry, sleep, posture, teeth grinding, pain, or medication effects. Tracking where the tension occurs and how long it lasts can help the care team understand whether it follows changes in mood, daily circumstances, or treatment.

### 19. Tension
Today, did you have any sudden chills or hot flashes around the times you felt low, stressed, or overwhelmed?

Slider: `0 — Not at all` to `10 — Extremely`

#### Why are we asking you this?

Sudden temperature sensations can accompany changes in the body’s stress response, but they may also be related to medication, hormones, illness, or the environment. Recording when they happen and what else is happening at the same time gives the care team useful context without assuming a single cause.

### 20. Optional closing note

After the slider questions, offer an optional text or voice field:

> **Is there anything else about today that you want to remember or want your care team to understand? (i.e., Events that could explain behavioural changes, independent from medications.**

This field should always remain optional.

## Experience guidelines

- Present one question per screen.
- Keep **Why are we asking you this?** available beneath every question as an expandable explanation.
- Do not preselect a slider value.
- Allow the patient to skip a question without moving the slider.
- Use gentle transitions and avoid red warning colors except when presenting genuine safety information.
- Do not describe scores as good, bad, successful, or failed.
- Do not celebrate lower symptom scores or criticize higher ones.
- Confirm completion with neutral language such as: **Thank you for checking in today. Your answers have been recorded.**
- Clearly tell patients whether their answers are monitored in real time.

## Clinical review requirement

This is a custom monitoring questionnaire, not a validated diagnostic instrument. The final wording, slider interpretation, alert thresholds, medication-specific follow-ups, and safety response process must be reviewed and validated by qualified clinicians before the survey is used with patients.

The safety workflow should be designed with an established resource such as the [NIMH Ask Suicide-Screening Questions Toolkit](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-conducted-at-nimh/asq-toolkit-materials). A continuous slider can make the daily experience more approachable, but it should never replace validated follow-up questions or professional clinical judgment.
